Supplementary Methods
 
S1 Study 1
 
S1.1 LLM prompt
-
tuning procedures/dataset generation
 
We drew on conversational excerpts from a prior, in
-
progress research project by 
Anderson and colleagues (Anderson et al., personal communication, 2026), which involved 
synchronous conversations between a human sharer and a curated chatbot about stressful 
life 
experiences. In two experiments (
N
 
rticipants had a conversation with one (Study 1) or 
all of the four (Study 2) customized chatbots prompted to express one of three primary 
components of empathy
—
perspective
-
taking, emotion sharing, or compa
ssion
—
or a neutral, 
non
-
empathic control bot that exhibited none of these features. These interactions focused on 
stressors across multiple life domains, including academic and occupational demands, health 
concerns, and social difficulties. The present stu
dy builds on these materials and procedures, and 
details of the chatbot tuning process are provided below.
 
The development and calibration of the chatbots proceeded in several stages. First, the 
authors conducted a review of theoretical and empirical literature on empathy to identify the 
defining features of each component
1,2
, with particular attention to elements unique to each facet 
(e.g., reflection on understanding what the sharer is expressing for perspective
-
taking; resonating 
with the emotions the sharer expressed; motivational concern for alleviating distress in 
compas
sion
3
). Based on this literature, de
tailed instructional prompts were constructed and 
hosted on Steamlit. These prompts specified the target empathic behaviors for each chatbot and 
explicitly delineated prohibited responses. For example, the emotion
-
sharing chatbot was 
instructed to resonate
 
with and validate the participant’s expressed feelings (e.g., “I hear how 

===== PAGE BREAK =====

overwhelming this must be for you”), while refraining from perspective
-
taking, interpretive 
inferences, problem
-
solving, or attempts to alleviate distress.
 
These initial prompts were iteratively refined using OpenAI’s ChatGPT interface, 
specifically the GPT feature, which allows for the customization of conversational agents. 
Prompts were repeatedly tested to assess whether chatbot responses adhered to the in
tended 
empathic profile. When deviations occurred
—
such as instances of hallucination, blending of 
empathic components, or unsolicited advice
—
additional constraints and clarifications were 
introduced. This process involved comparing responses generated thro
ugh ChatGPT with outputs 
produced by the Steamlit
-
hosted bots, and revising prompt instructions to improve consistency to 
the target construct. This iterative cycle continued until the responses reliably reflected the 
intended empathic facet and minimized 
contamination from other components, based on the 
author’s own assessments.
 
Once satisfactory behavioral consistency was achieved, the finalized chatbots were 
integrated into a Qualtrics survey. Participants recruited through SONA systems
4
 
and 
CloudResearch Connect participant pool platforms were then invited to write about a stressful 
experience and interact with the bots in real time, discussing their personally relevant stressful 
experiences over a structured interaction period of 10 (St
udy 1) or 5 (Study 2) minutes. Based on 
their conversations, participants reported on their p
erceptions of the chatbot responses’ empathy 
and responsiveness. 
 
In the present study, these conversational excerpts were used as stimuli to examine the 
relationship between empathic responding and perceived sycophancy. We further explored 
whether different forms of expressed empathy systematically shaped participants’ 
ratings of 
sycophancy.

S2 Studies 2 and 3
 
S2.1 Dataset generation
 
The dataset used in Studies 2
–
3 was drawn from a prior study on AI sycophancy. In that 
study, 838 participants each held a conversation with a chatbot that was experimentally 
manipulated to respond either sycophantically or disagreeably. Participants were 
instructed to 
share an opinion with the chatbot and were randomly assigned to discuss either a personal 
conflict (e.g., disputes with coworkers or family members) or a moral/political issue of their 
choosing (e.g., abortion, immigration).
 
The sycophantic chatbot was instructed to enthusiastically validate the user's 
perspective
—
making them feel heard, understood, and correct
—
while avoiding 
counterarguments or challenges. The disagreeable chatbot was instructed to challenge the user's 
opinio
n by presenting compelling alternative viewpoints, directly questioning their position, and 
offering evidence
-
based counterarguments.
 
The resulting dataset comprised 838 conversations distributed across four conditions: 
sycophantic
–
moral (
n
 
sagreeable
–
moral (
n
 
ycophantic
–
personal conflict (
n
 
= 
221), and disagreeable
–
personal conflict (
n
 
= 200).
 
Manipulation checks in the original study confirmed that the experimental conditions 
were perceived as intended. Compared to the disagreeable chatbot, the sycophantic chatbot was 
rated as significantly more agreeable, 
t
(758) = 9.87, 
p
< .001, more validating, 
t
(768) = 10.94, 
p
 
< 
.001, and less open to new perspectives, 
t
(839) = −4.99, 
p
 
< .001. Conversely, the disagreeable 
chatbot was rated as more disagreeable, 
t
(790) = 8.33, 
p
 
< .001, and more challenging, 
t
(826) = 
12.12, 
p
 
< .001.
